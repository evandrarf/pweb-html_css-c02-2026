What Was the
Manhattan Project?

The Manhattan Project was a top-secret research and development undertaking during World War II that produced the world's first nuclear weapons. It was led by the United States, with support from the United Kingdom and Canada.

Officially launched in 1942, the project spanned three years and involved more than 130,000 people across dozens of secret sites. At its core was a single, terrifying question: "Could a self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction be weaponized?"

J. Robert Oppenheimer was appointed Scientific Director of the Los Alamos Laboratory in New Mexico — the project's central weapon-design facility — in 1943. His brilliance and leadership proved to be ultimately decisive, despite early doubts from military officials.

Project
Phases

The Manhattan Project unfolded across four major interconnected phases:

  1. Early Research (1939–1941) — Physicists including Einstein and Szilard informed President Roosevelt of the possibility of a nuclear bomb through the Einstein–Szilard Letter.
  2. Formation & Mobilization (1942) — The official program launched under the Army Corps of Engineers. General Groves was appointed military commander.
  3. Research & Material Production (1943–1944) — Uranium enrichment at Oak Ridge and plutonium production at Hanford ran in parallel. Oppenheimer led weapon design at Los Alamos.
  4. Testing & Deployment (1945) — The Trinity Test succeeded on July 16, 1945. Little Boy was dropped on Hiroshima (August 6) and Fat Man on Nagasaki (August 9), ending World War II.

The Science Behind the Bomb

Two nuclear weapon design paths developed simultaneously.

01

Gun-Type Design

Little Boy Uranium-235

A simpler design: one sub-critical mass of uranium is fired into another using conventional explosives, triggering a fission chain reaction.

  • Required no prior testing
  • Dropped on Hiroshima, August 6, 1945
  • Yield equivalent to ±15,000 tons of TNT
02

Implosion Design

Fat Man Plutonium-239

A more complex design: explosive lenses surround a plutonium core and detonate inward symmetrically, achieving a super-critical density in a fraction of a second.

  • Required prior testing — the Trinity Test
  • Dropped on Nagasaki, August 9, 1945
  • Yield equivalent to ±21,000 tons of TNT
03

Trinity Test

July 16, 1945 New Mexico

The world's first nuclear detonation. Oppenheimer watched the explosion from an observation bunker and recalled a passage from the Bhagavad Gita:

"Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds."

A moral legacy still debated to this day.

Legacy &
Moral Reckoning

The success of the Manhattan Project brought an end to World War II, but also opened an era that Oppenheimer himself described as "the destroyer of worlds."

The project's long-term consequences include:

  • Over 200,000 lives lost due to the bombings of Hiroshima & Nagasaki
  • The beginning of the Cold War and the US–Soviet nuclear arms race
  • Establishment of the AEC (Atomic Energy Commission) to regulate nuclear technology
  • Oppenheimer's security clearance revoked in 1954 amid political allegations
  • His name was finally cleared by the US Department of Energy in December 2022
